01The question is a volatility question, not a percentile question

The percentile is pure arithmetic once the margin and the volatility are fixed. Reading it backwards shows what belief about volatility each answer corresponds to. Volatility here means the standard deviation of the total earned reserve requirement, as a percentage of the best estimate.

So an expectation of the 70th–80th is exactly the belief that portfolio reserve volatility is 10–15% of the best estimate. The model says 8.1%. The rest of this note asks which of the two is wrong.

An external check

The Solvency II standard formula sets reserve-risk standard deviations of 8–20% by segment, and all three non-proportional reinsurance segments — casualty, marine/aviation/transport and property — sit at 20%. Its inter-segment correlations run from 0.25 to 0.50; zero does not appear anywhere. Applying that shape to this book:

02The aggregation is dominated by two classes and three cells

With classes drawn independently the variances add, so each class's contribution is its own standard deviation squared. The result is extreme concentration — and it is the mechanism behind the skew you sensed.

Share of earned IBNR — how much money is at stake Share of the modelled variance — how much risk the model assigns

Energy carries 46% of the earned IBNR and 97% of the variance. Life carries 40% of the IBNR and 1.5% of the variance. Because the statutory margin is spread across the whole book, the life block contributes 23% of the margin and almost none of the risk it is supposed to be covering. That mismatch mechanically buys percentile.

At cell level the concentration is starker still: of 14 classes and 76 simulated class-and-year cells, three cells carry 80% of the modelled variance.

The ranking is unambiguous. Correlation between classes is worth 7–11 points; recalibrating the life CoVs is worth half a point. That is not because the life CoVs are defensible — they are not — but because life contributes almost no variance either way, so changing it cannot move the aggregate.

04Where each class's percentile comes from

For a class, the booked percentile is Φ(z) with

z = u × √neff ÷ CoV

where u is the statutory uplift on the earned IBNR, neff is the effective number of underwriting years (one divided by the sum of the squared year shares) and CoV is the class loss-ratio coefficient of variation. Case reserves and SIBNR cancel out of z entirely, because they are modelled as certain.

Two things to note. First, the √neff term is pure artefact: it is there only because the underwriting years of a class are drawn independently, which divides a class's volatility by up to two. For reserve risk that is the wrong assumption — a change in development pattern or in reserving basis hits every open year at once. Second, the CoV in Inputs!Q:R is an ultimate-loss-ratio volatility, i.e. an underwriting-risk measure, being used here as a reserve-risk measure. They are different quantities, and the substitution happens to look plausible for energy and implausible for life.

Correction to an earlier draft of this note

In the first version of these diagnostics the case-and-SIBNR component was drawn independently of everything else, which understated it: that component should take the portfolio-wide shock like any other part of the book. Corrected, candidate E lands at the 73.4th rather than the 75.9th, with a standard deviation of 17.7% rather than 15.6%. Every table in this note now uses the corrected treatment, and it is the one implemented in the tool. Whether the case component correlates with the IBNR of its own class on top of that turns out to make almost no difference to the aggregate.

Recommendation — candidate E

ρ between classes 0.25 · case reserves and SIBNR at a 10% CoV · life CoV floored at 0.25

Portfolio 73.4th with a portfolio σ of 17.7%, every class between the 66th and the 80th, and no component that cannot be defended in a regulatory conversation: 0.25 is the Annex IV floor, not a judgement; case reserve uncertainty is real and currently set to zero; and a 25% CoV floor is still below the standard-formula σ for every reinsurance segment.

The one component with no external anchor is the coefficient of variation placed on case reserves and SIBNR. It is the lever to turn if the standard deviation is judged too conservative:

At zero — the workbook's own basis — the standard deviation sits inside the standard-formula range and the portfolio reads the 76.4th. At 10% it is above that range, which is a deliberate conservatism rather than a calibration error: case reserve run-off risk is real and the standard formula embeds it inside a single segment sigma rather than separating it.

If a lower standard deviation is preferred, candidate C is the same package with the class correlation at 0.20 instead of 0.25 — 74.3th at 16.3%. Below 0.15 the calibration is no longer supportable by reference to the standard formula.

07What to change in the tool

  1. Defaults that are defensible rather than convenient. ρ between classes to 0.25, and a nested correlation structure — between business groups, between classes inside a group, between years inside a class — so that energy and life are not forced to share one number. This is the change that moves the headline.
  2. A concentration diagnostic. The two tables in section 02, live in the tool: share of IBNR against share of variance by class, and the top cells. This is what makes a result like the 87th self-explaining instead of surprising.
  3. CoV governance per class. Show the number of usable observations next to each CoV; offer the fitted value, a credibility-weighted value shrunk toward a peer-group benchmark with weight n/(n+k), and a manual override with a comment field. Flag any class whose CoV rests on fewer than five years.
  4. Parameter uncertainty as an option. Rescale each class's CoV per iteration by √((n−1)/χ²n−1), capped. This is the statistically correct answer to a three-point estimate and it widens the tail precisely where the data is thin.
  5. Risk on case reserves and SIBNR. A separate CoV for the 52% of the book that is currently certain, correlated with its class.
  6. Per-class distribution family. One global lognormal for both energy and life is hard to justify. Note the direction of travel, though: with the mean and σ held fixed, a heavier tail lowers the median and therefore raises the booked percentile. A large-loss overlay for energy is about tail realism at the 99.5th, not about moving the headline down.
